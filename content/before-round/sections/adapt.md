Pre-round small talk can give you insights into how aggressive or passive your competitors may become once the round starts. Use those early impressions to start planning the roles you might take and the skills that will fit the group best.

### Read the group before the round

Aggressive students often come out swinging right away. They may try to show leadership by dominating the procedural elements of a round: setting an agenda, controlling the timekeeping, and keeping the group focused.

Quieter students who participate less in pre-round small talk often carry that into the round. They may be the students you need to invite and encourage to contribute more.

These are not universal rules that predict how someone will behave. Still, pre-round interactions can often help you form useful early impressions of the competitors at your table.

### Anticipate the level of competition

Preliminary rounds commonly have more students who are less aggressive. In those groups, taking responsibility for procedural contributions can be advantageous. Students who set agendas, keep time, and keep the group on track often appear as leaders to judges and earn the best ranks.

At more competitive tournaments, such as Sections and State, or in a final round, you will often find more aggressive students. Several people may want to control the same procedural elements. A strategy that works in a preliminary round may not be the best fit for that group.

### Plan the role that fits

If you find yourself in a highly competitive round with many students who want to control procedural elements, be prepared to give up those roles and focus more on **substantive contributions**. Bring useful ideas, evidence, explanations, and connections to the discussion rather than competing for every procedural opportunity.

Conversely, a group with more passive students gives you more opportunities for **procedural contributions**. Be ready to set agendas, keep time, and gatekeep the round by inviting and encouraging participation so the group can move forward.

Rely on your early intuition to start game planning, then adjust as the round develops. Draw on the procedural and substantive skills you have already learned to fit the group dynamics.
