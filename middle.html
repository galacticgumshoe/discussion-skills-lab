Evidence Is Your Strongest Contribution

RELEVANCE→EVIDENCE→EXPLANATION→CONNECTION

Expand all four sections to examine the parts of a strong contribution.

Relevance

Follow the current conversation. Address the group's task rather than repeating or abruptly changing the subject.

Save a finding for a later agenda item if it does not fit the present discussion.
Evidence

Cite at least the publication or publisher and publication date. Add the author or article title when available.

“According to [publication], in [month/year], [finding]. This matters because [connection].” This is a template, not a real citation.
Explanation

Go beyond stating a fact or statistic.

Explain why the finding matters and how it could influence the group's decision.
Connection

Link your point to another speaker, the agenda, or a developing solution.

Make explicit how your evidence advances the group's task.

Research and anecdotes have different jobs

Research supplies externally supported evidence. Personal stories can make an issue concrete, but one anecdote does not prove a universal experience. Use relevant, credible evidence to support factual claims.

Strong Discussants Do More Than Talk

Expand all four strategies, then work through the group decisions.

Take notes

Record major topics, evidence, sources, proposals, agreements, and unresolved questions. Credit others' ideas and look up regularly to remain engaged.

Topic: Transportation barriers
Maya: bus routes
Jordan: cost concerns
Open question: Which option is realistic?
Manage time

Track the round and agreed agenda periods. Timing protects the opportunity to finish the deliverable, but cannot replace substantive participation.

Include others

At a transition, ask: “Before we move on, are there any other perspectives we should consider?” Broad invitations avoid repeatedly singling out quieter participants.

Focus the group

Respectfully refer to the task or agenda: “Could we return to the barriers our plan needs to address?” Propose a constructive next step.

How You Say It Matters

Expand all three sections to review the communication strategies.

Body language

Maintain attentive posture, nod naturally, and look up from notes. Avoid eye rolling, exaggerated sighs, or distracting habits. Direct attention to fellow discussants rather than the judge.

These are practical coaching suggestions, not individual official rules.

Professional language

Speak concisely, precisely, and respectfully within your assigned role. Reduce fillers, excessive slang, and unrelated references.

Too casual: “Guys, this idea is kinda bad.”

Professional: “I have a concern about whether this proposal is realistic.”

Voice and volume

Speak clearly enough for a noisy room and finish sentences audibly. A fading ending can be mistaken for an invitation to take a turn. Steady volume does not mean shouting.

05 / 07 · About 5 minutes

When Everyone Wants to Speak

Balance your opportunity to contribute with respect for the group.

Interactive strategy spectrum

Expand all four strategies. None is an automatic default.

Continue assertively

What it means: Continue a brief point when voices overlap.

Potential advantage: May preserve a timely point.

Main risk: Repeatedly talking over others can appear domineering.

“Let me finish this connection briefly, and then I’d like to hear other perspectives.”
Request to go first

What it means: Acknowledge the other speaker and ask to make your point first.

Potential advantage: Preserves an opportunity while recognizing another person.

Main risk: Repeated requests can control the floor. Honor the promised next turn.

“Would you mind if I finish this point first? I’d like to hear you afterward.”
Yield and request the next turn

What it means: Let the other person go first, then ask to contribute.

Potential advantage: Builds trust and balances participation.

Main risk: The discussion may move on before your point fits.

“Go ahead. I’d like to build on that afterward.”
Claim a direct connection

What it means: Explain why your point responds immediately to the previous speaker.

Potential advantage: Preserves continuity.

Main risk: Repeated urgency claims can dominate the floor.

“Could I respond briefly to that concern before we change topics?”

Prevent an interruption

↻ Flip card

Maintain steady volume through your sentence so others know your thought is not complete.

↻ Flip card

Middle Stage Quick Reference

01Contribute substance

  • Share relevant research and insights frequently.
  • Verbally cite publication and date; explain why evidence matters.
  • Use anecdotes to illustrate, not replace, evidence.
  • Avoid repetition and unrelated detours.

02Support the group

  • Take notes on ideas, evidence, and their contributors; look up regularly.
  • Help track the round and agreed agenda periods.
  • Invite perspectives without repeatedly singling people out.
  • Redirect respectfully toward the WHAT and HOW of the task.

03Communicate professionally

  • Listen actively; avoid visible frustration and distracting habits.
  • Address fellow discussants, not the judge.
  • Use concise language suited to your assigned role.
  • Speak clearly and maintain volume through sentence endings.

04Manage speaking conflicts

  • Continue assertively only with care; it risks dominance.
  • Request first turn and honor your promise to yield.
  • Yield and request the next turn when appropriate.
  • Explain a timely, direct connection when requesting a turn.
  • If interrupted, politely finish and give the promised speaker a turn.

05Your goal

  • Contribute evidence. Build on others. Keep the group moving toward a solution.
