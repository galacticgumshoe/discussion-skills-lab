# Discussion Skills Lab — New Module Specification: Before the Round

**Status:** Revised instructional-design proposal for review; not authorization to commit or publish.
**Curriculum placement:** Lesson 4, following Opening Stage, Middle Stage, and Final Stage. This is a refinement module taught in the fourth instructional session, not a prerequisite to the three foundational lessons.

## 1. Purpose and learner outcomes

**Title:** Before the Round  
**Module ID:** `before-round`  
**URL:** `before-round.html`  
**New storage key:** `dsl:before-round:v1`  
**Estimated duration:** 15–20 minutes.  
**Audience:** Grades 9–12, MSHSL Discussion students who have completed introductory instruction on the Opening, Middle, and Final Stages.
**Instructional position:** Fourth homepage lesson and fourth teaching session. Students already understand what a Discussion task is, that the task initiates the round, and the basic progression from task analysis through substantive discussion to synthesis. Do not reteach those fundamentals or make this lesson a technical prerequisite for earlier lessons.

**Opening framing (student-facing):** “You’ve learned how to open a Discussion round, contribute during the middle, and help your group reach a strong conclusion. Now focus on what happens before the round begins. Small decisions about preparation, task access, seating, and connecting with other participants can help you enter the discussion ready to collaborate.”

**Instructional scope:** This is an applied readiness and competitive-polish lesson. Assume prior familiarity with the term *task* and the round’s three stages. Include only a brief reminder that task distribution marks the start of the round under the applicable tournament procedure; focus on how students prepare for different distribution formats.

At completion, students can: (1) prepare necessary round equipment and sensible tournament-day extras; (2) respond appropriately to electronically released or paper-distributed tasks; (3) arrive and set up with attention to seating instructions and connectivity; (4) start low-pressure, inclusive pre-round conversations; (5) notice group interaction patterns without making premature judgments or treating other students as adversaries; and (6) follow a practical readiness checklist.

**Source fidelity:** Preserve the four major source topics: Equipment (p. 1), Task Publishing (p. 2), Arrive At Your Round Early (p. 3), and Icebreakers (pp. 4–5). Retain examples of icebreakers and the strategic observation/adaptation concept. Recast the labels “allies and opponents,” “Aggressive Project Manager,” and “Conciliatory Unifier” as **possible observable behaviors, not fixed identities or predictions of competitive success**. Preserve the coaching idea of adapting constructively to the group. Treat the source's examples of specific tournaments, seating requirements and task timing as *historical/local coaching context*, not verified universal current rules. Instruct students to follow current tournament and coach directions. Do not introduce additional MSHSL regulations.

## 2. Proposed learning flow and exact activities

All ordinary sections unlock sequentially through meaningful activity completion; the Quick Reference is always available. Completion credit is permanent once earned. Section names and IDs below are proposed stable IDs; do not change them after release.

### Section 1 — Ready to go (`pack`), ~3 minutes

**Lead copy:** “A successful round starts before you enter the room. Pack the items you need at the table, then think about what will help you through a long tournament day.”

**Teach:** At the round: fully charged internet-capable device, charger as backup, readable name tent (pronouns or speaker code optional), pen/pencil and notebook, refillable water bottle. Phone/hotspot is useful but **not required**; carry phone charger if using it. For the tournament day, not at the round table: snacks, lunch if desired, and warm layers for cold buses or buildings. The source recommends keeping food out of rounds; avoid presenting this as a universal formal rule.

**Interaction `pack-checklist` (checklist):** “I have a charged research device and backup charger”; “I can display a readable name tent”; “I have something to take notes with”; “I have water”; “I have checked whether I need a hotspot backup.” These are self-confirmation checks for a personal preparation plan, not a claim that every item is mandatory. **Gate:** all five checks selected. Allow students to uncheck afterward without revoking earned section credit.

**Optional static callout:** “Tournament bag: snacks/lunch and warm layers belong in your between-round plan.”

### Section 2 — How the task arrives (`task-access`), ~3 minutes

**Lead copy:** “In Opening Stage, you learned that the round begins when the task is issued under the tournament’s procedure. Now consider what you can do before that moment. Ask your coach how tasks will be distributed, because preparation differs when the task is available early.”

**Teach:** Briefly connect this topic to the familiar Opening Stage concept of receiving and analyzing the task; do not add a full task-analysis tutorial. Electronic publication may involve a shared online document and can occur before the round; open the announced source when available, monitor updates, and use permitted advance time to analyze the task and organize relevant research. Paper distribution may happen in the room immediately before the round begins; be ready to read and analyze promptly. The original document describes judges distributing face-down copies and starting the round as papers are turned over; label this as an example of a paper-distribution procedure, not a universal rule.

**Interaction `task-scenarios` (question-sequence, two questions):**

1. **ID `early-release`** Prompt: “Your coach shares an electronic task before the round. What is the best use of the available time?” Options: `ignore` — “Wait until the round to open it”; `prepare` **CORRECT** — “Read the task, identify its deliverable, and organize relevant research”; `script` — “Write a speech to deliver regardless of the group’s ideas.” Feedback: “An early task gives you a chance to understand the group's problem and prepare useful evidence. Stay flexible: the final product belongs to the group.”
2. **ID `paper-release`** Prompt: “The tournament will distribute a paper task when the round begins. What should you do beforehand?” Options: `guess` — “Assume the task and prepare a fixed proposal”; `ready` **CORRECT** — “Have your materials ready and be prepared to analyze the task when it is distributed”; `demand` — “Insist on receiving the task early.” Feedback: “You cannot rely on advance access. Arriving prepared lets you focus on understanding the task when it becomes available.”

**Gate:** earn credit on both questions. Use the existing sequence profile that supports answer exploration and persistent earned credit; verify its actual compatibility before choosing profile.

### Section 3 — Arrive, orient, connect (`arrive`), ~3 minutes

**Lead copy:** “Find your assigned room or table early enough to get settled before the round starts.”

**Teach:** Rounds may be in gymnasiums or classrooms. Confirm tournament-specific seating instructions; the source describes assigned seating at Sections/State, but current requirements must be checked. If seating is flexible, choose a location where you can hear and be heard; being near the judge is presented in the source as a coaching suggestion, not a rule. Set out the name tent and materials, verify internet access, and use an approved hotspot backup if needed.

**Interaction `arrival-decisions` (question-sequence, two questions):**

1. **ID `seat-order`** Prompt: “You arrive early and see that the round instructions specify a seating order. What should guide your seat choice?” Options: `judge` — “Sit nearest the judge regardless”; `instructions` **CORRECT** — “Follow the seating instructions for this round”; `friend` — “Sit beside someone you know even if that changes the order.” Feedback: “Tournament directions take priority over seating strategy. When seating is flexible, choose a place where you can participate effectively.”
2. **ID `wifi`** Prompt: “Your research device cannot connect to the room's Wi-Fi. What is the best response?” Options: `backup` **CORRECT** — “Troubleshoot promptly and use an available, permitted hotspot or other backup”; `wait` — “Ignore it until you need to cite research”; `interrupt` — “Ask the group to stop later while you fix it.” Feedback: “Checking connectivity before the round reduces interruptions. A hotspot is a useful backup, not a required piece of equipment.”

**Gate:** earn both question IDs.

### Section 4 — Build a cooperative start (`connect`), ~4 minutes

**Lead copy:** “Discussion depends on cooperation. Before the clock starts, a friendly conversation can make it easier to exchange ideas with students from other schools.”

**Teach:** Purpose of icebreakers: establish rapport, help everyone feel included, and set a collaborative tone. The source suggests Two Truths and a Lie, Have You Ever?, Would You Rather?, Find 10 Things in Common, or ordinary small talk. Not every group needs a formal game. Keep it brief, inclusive, and low pressure. Avoid divisive topics such as politics and religion, and avoid sharing identifying school or hometown details when following the source's coaching advice. Be mindful of the judge and others preparing nearby. Never pressure someone to disclose personal information.

**Interaction `icebreaker-cards` (flashcards, three cards):**

- `easy-choice` — Front: “Would You Rather?” Back: “Offer two light, low-stakes choices, such as cake or pie, and invite others to answer.”
- `common-ground` — Front: “Find Things in Common” Back: “Invite shared interests or experiences without requiring anyone to reveal personal details.”
- `casual-start` — Front: “Simple Small Talk” Back: “A friendly question about the day, weather, or a shared noncontroversial interest can be enough.”

**Interaction `icebreaker-choice` (question-sequence, one question):**

- **ID `inclusive-start`** Prompt: “A few students are quietly setting up. Which opener best supports a comfortable, collaborative atmosphere?” Options: `politics` — “Ask everyone to debate a controversial political issue”; `light` **CORRECT** — “Offer an optional, light question and leave room for people to finish setting up”; `identity` — “Ask each person to identify their school and hometown.” Feedback: “Low-pressure invitations build rapport while respecting other students' privacy and preparation time.”

**Gate:** explore all three cards and earn `inclusive-start`.

### Section 5 — Observe and adapt (`adapt`), ~3 minutes

**Lead copy:** “Early interactions can offer clues about how a group communicates, but they do not define anyone. Watch for behaviors, stay open-minded, and choose responses that help the group collaborate.”

**Teach:** Source distinguishes students who take commanding control from students who listen, include others, and unify ideas. Present these as *temporary behaviors*, not personality types or guaranteed predictors of results. If one student dominates, support a fair process and invite others in rather than escalating a power struggle. If peers are already collaborative, contribute substance and reinforce their inclusive behavior. Adapt to the group without stereotyping or forming adversarial camps.

**Interaction `adapt-scenarios` (question-sequence, two questions):**

1. **ID `dominant-peer`** Prompt: “Before the round, one student speaks over others and starts proposing an agenda. What is the most constructive takeaway?” Options: `label` — “Decide they will be an opponent for the whole round”; `adapt` **CORRECT** — “Notice the behavior, remain open-minded, and be ready to support an inclusive process”; `compete` — “Plan to interrupt them first.” Feedback: “Observation can help you prepare, but it is not a reliable label. Respond to the group's actual needs once the round begins.”
2. **ID `inclusive-peer`** Prompt: “Another student listens attentively and invites quieter people into a casual conversation. How should you respond?” Options: `support` **CORRECT** — “Build on that inclusive tone and prepare to contribute collaboratively”; `withdraw` — “Assume they will handle all group leadership”; `ally` — “Treat them as a permanent ally regardless of the task.” Feedback: “Inclusive behavior is worth reinforcing, but every participant still needs to contribute thoughtfully to the shared task.”

**Gate:** earn both question IDs.

### Section 6 — Readiness check (`knowledge-check`), ~3 minutes

**Lead copy:** “Apply your pre-round routine to a few realistic tournament situations.”

**Interaction `readiness-quiz` (question-sequence, five questions):**

1. `equipment` — Prompt: “Which item is described as useful but not required?” Options: `notes` — “Something to take notes with”; `name` — “A readable name tent”; `hotspot` **CORRECT** — “A phone hotspot.” Feedback: “A hotspot can help when Wi-Fi is unreliable, but the source identifies it as optional.”
2. `advance-task` — Prompt: “An electronic task is available ahead of time. What should you do?” Options: `analyze` **CORRECT** — “Analyze it and prepare relevant research”; `ignore` — “Ignore it until the round starts”; `speech` — “Memorize a fixed speech.” Feedback: “Advance access is an opportunity to prepare without preempting the group's decisions.”
3. `seating` — Prompt: “The tournament specifies an assigned seating order. What takes priority?” Options: `strategy` — “Your preferred seat near the judge”; `rules` **CORRECT** — “The current round's seating directions”; `friends` — “Sitting beside a friend.” Feedback: “Follow the directions for the specific tournament and round.”
4. `icebreaker` — Prompt: “What is the main purpose of pre-round small talk?” Options: `rapport` **CORRECT** — “Build rapport and a cooperative tone”; `rank` — “Rank classmates as allies or opponents”; `control` — “Take control before the timer starts.” Feedback: “Icebreakers prepare people to work together; they are not an extra competition.”
5. `observe` — Prompt: “Someone dominates the pre-round conversation. What is the best response?” Options: `assume` — “Assume they will always be difficult”; `adapt` **CORRECT** — “Observe without labeling and be ready to support inclusive participation”; `mirror` — “Dominate the next conversation yourself.” Feedback: “Respond to observable behaviors constructively, and leave room for people to surprise you.”

**Gate:** all five questions earn credit; show completion feedback and preserve answer exploration. The Quick Reference remains accessible before and after completion.

### Section 7 — Quick Reference (`quick-reference`, always available)

**Title:** Pre-Round Readiness Checklist. Use the existing `stacked-reference` layout with five concise groups:

1. **Pack for the round:** Charged research device; charger; readable name tent; note-taking tools; water; optional hotspot/phone charger.
2. **Plan for the day:** Snacks/lunch and warm layers for between rounds; check the tournament schedule.
3. **Find the task:** Confirm electronic versus paper distribution; if early electronic access is available, read the task and prepare research; otherwise be ready to analyze it promptly when issued.
4. **Arrive and set up:** Find the room/table early; follow seating directions; place materials; test Wi-Fi and backup.
5. **Connect respectfully:** Start friendly, optional small talk; include others; avoid divisive/personal topics; observe behavior without labeling people.

**Print:** Always-accessible Quick Reference; print the sheet alone using existing semantic reference hooks and `assets/css/print.css`. No separate duplicate checklist UI required on this screen.
